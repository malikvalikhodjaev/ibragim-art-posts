Before a large abstract painting, the eye first detects a general tension: red flashes among light planes, black bands bring the composition together, diagonals impart movement. The painting makes itself felt before we have begun to explain it. This impression is formed by very specific artistic decisions—the relationship of colour patchs, the character of lines, and the distribution of large and small forms.

These relationships are particularly rewarding to explore in the paintings of Ibragim Valikhodjaev. The artist works with portraits, landscapes, still lifes, and scenes of everyday life; in his abstract canvases, he explores the independent expressiveness of colour and geometry. The solo exhibition "New View," which opened in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, on November 15, 2024, offers a chance to explore this language in a large format. The Academy of Arts of Uzbekistan announced 28 new works, ranging in size from three to six meters.

For Valikhodjaev, "New View" became an exploration of the abstract language of painting. He was fascinated by the possibility of constructing an image through colour, geometry, and compositional relationships: what happens when the outlines of objects give way to independent forms, how expressiveness changes with increasing scale, and what tension arises between adjacent colour fields. The exhibition reveals the artist's curiosity about this new direction and his desire to expand his own artistic possibilities.

## Composition: How Parts Form a Whole

Composition is the structure of a painting, the relative positioning of its parts. Valikhodjaev's geometric forms vary in size, tilt, and density. A large, light plane is perceived differently than several small, dark areas; a rectangle maintains stability, while a diagonal introduces movement. Their juxtaposition creates an inner balance that can remain tense and fluid.

In the works shown in the exhibition video above, dark borders in places clearly separate red, yellow, white, and grey fields. The outline here functions as an independent element: it connects distant parts, emphasises the rotation of form, and draws the eye to linger at the junction of two planes. Therefore, both the colour areas themselves and the lines between them are important.

The compositional centre doesn't necessarily coincide with the middle of the canvas. It could be a bright spot near the edge or a hub of several intersecting directions. After the initial impression, it's helpful to see how the other parts support this accent: responding with a similar colour, balancing it with a dark mass, and continuing the diagonal movement. This reveals the connection between the individual fragments.

## Colour: how adjacent fields affect one another

A painting’s palette is more than a list of pigments: it is the relationship between its colours. It's determined not by the specific colours used, but by their relationships. Red next to black appears focused and sharp; the same red, juxtaposed with white and grey surfaces, appears more spacious. The size of the spot, the saturation of the colour, and the lightness of the adjacent areas all contribute to its expressive power.

In geometric painting, these relationships are clearly visible because the colour fields have clear boundaries. A small, bright area can hold the attention just as well as a large, calm surface. Multiple repetitions of the same colour connect different parts of the canvas. This distribution helps us perceive the painting as a whole, even when its surface is dense with numerous elements.

Colour can have an emotional connotation—anxious, celebratory, or focused. However, each work has its own structure. To understand it more precisely, one should look at where exactly the contrasts are concentrated and how they interact with the lines. The impression is born from the composition of the entire painting and is then interpreted personally by the viewer.

## Rhythm and pauses

Rhythm in painting arises from the repetition and variation of forms. Several vertical lines create one sequence, while inclined planes create another. Between them, there are intervals: narrow gaps, wide fields, areas of calmer colour. They give the image the necessary breathing space and allow the most intense fragments to stand out.

At the "New View" exhibition, the alternation of large geometries and small details is particularly noticeable. From a distance, the overall structure is discernible; upon closer inspection, localized combinations are revealed. The gaze sometimes takes in a broad surface, then lingers on a particular connection of lines. It is precisely this shift in attention that makes the viewing experience prolonged and meaningful.

A single composition can suggest several directions of movement. The red spot draws the viewer in first, the dark outline leads to the edge, and the repeating light shape returns to the centre. These transitions suggest the painting's dynamism, even though the image itself is static. The artistic movement here is created by the relationships between the elements.

In this search, each canvas offers a distinct solution. The artist alters the relationship between large and small forms, thickens lines or leaves wide pauses, and tests the power of colour accents in new environments. It's interesting to consider such works as a sequence of artistic experiments: the common theme of abstraction opens up a multitude of ways to organise surfaces and evoke visual experience.

## Scale and personal perception

A canvas several meters wide engages the viewer differently than a smaller work. From a distance, the overall composition can be discerned; up close, a single colour field occupies a significant portion of the visual field. One is forced to change position, move away, and then move closer. Perceiving the painting involves one's movement in front of the canvas.

Photography and video help to visualize this experience: the figures of visitors, the height of the walls, and the doorways provide cues. The screen, however, minimizes the work and smooths out surface differences. The video above allows one to judge the exhibition's structure and composition of the paintings, while the texture, colour intensity, and physical scale are more fully revealed by an encounter with the original.

An introduction to abstraction begins with a careful look. It's enough to notice the main colour patch, observe the supporting forms, and follow several directions. Gradually, the initial impression gains concrete ground. The painting retains the possibility of multiple interpretations, and the viewer acquires their own, more precise language for communicating with it.

## Composition as a series of artistic choices

In a 2012 profile article for San'at magazine, Oksana Kadysheva drew attention to the importance of composition in Valikhodjaev's work. The critic linked expressive colour to the artist's way of assembling individual motifs into a coherent image. This observation also helps us understand his later abstraction: a recognisable object may disappear, but the work of harmonizing the parts remains. A large field requires its own internal order—the precise placement of accents, the connections between distant forms, and the appropriate spacing.

This reading changes the familiar question of "what is depicted here?" Instead of searching for a hidden object, one can compare two adjacent planes: which is more stable, which directs movement, where the colour tension reaches its greatest intensity. Then, it's worth stepping back and examining how the discovered relationship functions as a whole. Abstraction unfolds through this shift in distance and attention. The viewer gradually discerns the independent solutions that make up the artist's language.

## About the artist

Ibragim Valikhodjaev is an artist and sculptor from Uzbekistan, an academician of the Academy of Arts of Uzbekistan, and the chairman of the Union of Artists of Uzbekistan. He also works as a curator, expert, teacher, and organiser of international art projects; he founded the Valikhodjaev Gallery House. [Biography, exhibitions, and CV](<https://ibragim-valikhodjaev.blogspot.com/2026/10/blog-post.html>) .

Ibragim Valikhodjaev's works are held in the Yu Shun Art Museum (China, Harbin), in the collection of the Maltepe Municipality (Turkey, Istanbul), and also in a private collection in Moscow, Russia.
