In "The Harbour at Perast," nearby boats, stone houses, and the distant mountains are combined into multiple planes. Shown nearby is another view of the same town by Valikhodjaev—"Perast. Palm Trees on the Embankment." The two paintings from the Montenegrin series allow us to compare how this new motif transforms Valikhodjaev's composition and colour scheme.

## Foreground: boats organise the space

Several boats occupy the foreground of the painting, facing the viewer at different angles. Their light-coloured sides form rounded, elongated shapes; one bow juts forward, the other is set back. The repeating motif does not form a single row. Narrow patches of dark water are visible between the hulls, and these spaces are as important as the boats themselves: they separate adjacent silhouettes and draw the eye from one to the next.

The large boat on the right features noticeable coloured stripes and slender vertical posts. Compared to the wide, light-coloured side, they read as small, precise accents. The artist distributes details unevenly: what is essential to the spatial arrangement is emphasised, while the rest is incorporated into the overall pictorial structure. This maintains a compelling foreground and does not fragment into a list of details.

## The embankment leads the gaze into the depths

The stone embankment curves to the left of the boats, drawing the eye toward the distant part of the city. Its light surface approaches the hulls, but the direction is quite different: the boats turn toward the viewer, while the shoreline recedes. The meeting of these movements creates a sense of a lived-in port space.

Beyond the stone wall, houses rise up. Their facades, dark windows, and reddish roofs form a sequence of uneven rectangles. The buildings don't resemble a flat ornament: their sizes vary, individual walls are partially covered, and the roofs are at different levels. The dense urban cluster gradually retreats toward the water and mountains.

## Light stone and deep blue water

The colour scheme is based on the relationship between the light stone and whitish areas and the deep blue of the bay. The water is not painted with a single, even tone. Dark stripes near the boats, more open blue areas to the right, and small light traces change its tone across the entire surface. The boundary between the light side of the boat and the dark water becomes one of the most expressive areas of the painting.

The reddish roofs lend a warm accent to the architecture. Its area is relatively small, yet the colour brings the buildings together into a coherent group and separates them from the cold distance. Valikhodjaev's enduring interest is evident here: colour plays an equal role in the construction of the image, alongside line, scale, and the placement of form. The coastal motif lends this interest a different context than the rich red of rural landscapes.

## Mountains hold the background

Beyond the city and the water, mountain slopes unfold. They are painted more unified than the boats and nearby houses. The bluish and grey tones soften the distance, and the jagged line of the slopes connects it to the larger landscape. The painting's space therefore doesn't end at the embankment: the pier remains part of the larger bay.

The transition from the bright foreground to the comparatively calm distance sets the sequence of viewing. First, we distinguish individual boat hulls and their different angles, then we read the coastline and architecture, and finally the wider space. Several scales of observation are combined in a single, relatively small format: according to the archival caption, the painting measures 60 x 70 cm.

## Plein-air painting and a return to the motif

The work dates back to 2012, when Valikhodjaev participated in a plein air painting project in Montenegro. The exhibition "21 Days in Montenegro" was held that same year in Tashkent, Uzbekistan. This information helps us distinguish between the location of the creative trip and the subsequent exhibition. The painting itself allows us to see what the trip gave the artist: a new motif, a different relationship between architecture and water, and a different character of spatial transitions.

On the trip, observation takes on new contexts. The turning boats form the foreground, the embankment connects it to the city, and the cold distance expands the space. The finished painting preserves these relationships and allows us to return to what we saw in the exhibition hall.

## Landscape within a varied practice

Compared to the Namangan series, this pier exhibits a different tempo of painting. In the bazaar, movement emerges between a multitude of human figures; here, it is carried by the dissimilar boats, the shoreline, and the succession of buildings. What remains common is an interest in the rhythm of repeating forms and how each individual part contributes to the structure of the whole.

It is precisely these specific relationships that make the Montenegrin series a meaningful part of the artist's creative journey. Water, rock, and mountains offer a new set of challenges, and the artist responds to them through colour and composition. Returning from the general view to the boats in the foreground, it becomes clear how many expressive possibilities lie within a simple dock.

## Another look at Perast

In "Perast. Palms on the Embankment" (2012), the artist remains in the same city but shifts his focus. Instead of nearby boats and a pier receding into the distance, we see white walls, two large palm trees, and a dark blue sky. Cars and a green bench are visible at the bottom. A familiar urban environment is revealed through the encounter of large silhouettes and small signs of everyday life.

The dark treetops almost merge above the light facades. Their arched leaves create a directional movement, echoed by the smoother lines of the walls and roadway. Compared to "The Harbour at Perast," there's less of an extended distance here: the eye is drawn to a dense group of forms in the foreground. A comparison of the two original reproductions reveals not a finished "view of Montenegro," but two different compositional responses to the same city.

## Montenegrin experience in art criticism

In 2012, Oksana Kadysheva, in San'at magazine, examined Montenegrin works alongside Valikhodjaev's bazaar paintings. In her interpretation, the trip yielded silvery tones and varied shades of blue, distinct from the warm colours of familiar Uzbek subjects. It's important to understand this as a critic's observation of a specific series. The artist continued to work in various genres, and his new surroundings broadened his artistic scope.

Two reproductions of Perast provide a visual basis for this comparison. In "The Harbour at Perast," blue reveals the depth of the bay; in the palm-lined scene, it occupies the upper portion and anchors the silhouettes of the crowns. The light-coloured architecture in the first instance recedes into the distance; in the second, it approaches almost directly into the foreground. A single city offers different ways to organise colour and space. This diversity of responses makes the artist's work particularly evident: the location provides the motif, but the painting's structure must be redefined each time.

## About the artist

Ibragim Valikhodjaev is an artist and sculptor from Uzbekistan, an academician of the Academy of Arts of Uzbekistan, and the chairman of the Union of Artists of Uzbekistan. He also works as a curator, expert, teacher, and organiser of international art projects; he founded the Valikhodjaev Gallery House. [Biography, exhibitions, and CV](<https://ibragim-valikhodjaev.blogspot.com/2026/10/blog-post.html>) .

Ibragim Valikhodjaev's works are held in the Yu Shun Art Museum (China, Harbin), in the collection of the Maltepe Municipality (Turkey, Istanbul), and also in a private collection in Moscow, Russia.
