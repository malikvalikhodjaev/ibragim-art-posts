"Portrait of Dias Rakhmatov," "Portrait of Lena," and "Portrait of Sasha" differ in time, colour, and spatial construction. Comparison of the three archival reproductions allows us to explore the portrait genre through specific brushwork and compositional techniques.

## "Portrait of Dias Rakhmatov": face and wide light field

The elderly man's face is drawn close to the viewer. His dark headdress almost blends into the upper background, and the light plane behind his shoulders makes his head particularly prominent. The brushwork changes direction in the eyes, eyebrows, and folds of his face; his white hair and beard are composed of individual brushstrokes rather than a smooth outline.

The shoulders occupy a significant portion of the lower half of the painting. The dark grey and blue tones of the clothing support the figure's weight, leaving the face in the centre. The background is extremely simple, but not empty: a narrow ochre stripe at the top defines the boundary against which the silhouette of the headdress is related.

## Dias Rakhmatov in the studio: from encounter to portrait

**[Dias Rakhmatov](<https://uzbekkino.glob.uz/person/109>)** was an Uzbek stage and screen actor. These archive photographs show him in Valikhodjaev’s studio and the portrait taking shape. Beside the finished painting, they reveal the sitter’s presence, his position before the easel and the artist’s attention to face and pose.

## How a portrait directs the eye

In "Portrait of Dias Rakhmatov," the gaze begins with large-scale relationships between dark and light. It then moves to differences within the face: the turn of the head, the light brushstroke on the forehead, the dark areas around the eyes. The work can be viewed from the overall silhouette to individual brushstrokes, returning back to the whole.

The archival caption indicates the model's name, year, and size, but does not provide details of the session. The observed facial expression should not be turned into a ready-made biographical narrative. Expressiveness here is created by painterly means: the position of the head, the nature of the brushstrokes, and the relationship of the figure to the surrounding field.

## "Portrait of Lena": profile at the window

The 1987 work suggests a different scale of relationships. A woman in a reddish sweater sits in profile, closer to the left edge; to the right, a light wall and window remain. Unlike Dias Rakhmatov's large face, here the entire pose is important: the bent arms, the turn of the body, and the line of gaze connect the subject to the space of the room.

The cool greyish-blue sections of the wall provide a striking backdrop for the red garment. The light outside isn't described in detail, but the vertical boundaries of the window and windowsill establish spatial order. The warm figure, juxtaposed against the cold surroundings, becomes the focal point of the composition, without a dramatic gesture or detailed interior.

## Pause as part of the composition

Between the seated figure and the right edge of the painting, there remains a significant open space. This isn't a remnant of the background: its width determines the distance the eye travels from the figure to the window. Light verticals balance the silhouette, and the low horizontal line of the windowsill continues the direction of the arms.

If we mentally remove this space, the portrait loses some of its rhythm. Along with it, the subtle distinction between the concentrated figure and the open, light-coloured wall disappears. The work demonstrates how a person's image emerges not only in the face, but also in the proportions of the entire painting.

## "Portrait of Sasha": large planes of colour

In the 2006 portrait, the figure is again seated, but the language is different. The white blouse, brown waistcoat, blue skirt, greenish chair, and large leaves combine to form clear planes of colour. The face occupies a relatively small area. Its expressiveness is enhanced by the tilt of the head, the support of the hand, and the dark outline of the hair.

The plant on the left and the object on the right require no further explanation: they influence the composition. The leaves create an uneven, upward-facing silhouette, while the plane on the right encloses the space. The seated figure is positioned between them. The white sleeves draw the eye from the head to the arms and back again, while the blue skirt creates a large base for the image.

## Three Approaches to the Portrait

The difference between these portraits is noticeable even in the distance from the model. Dias Rakhmatov is shown up close; Lena's portrait is built around the figure and the window; in "Portrait of Sasha," the subject becomes part of a simplified colour scheme. For each composition, the artist chooses his own proportions, level of detail, and role of the background.

Together, works from 1987, 2006, and 2013 help us appreciate the breadth of Valikhodjaev's portraiture. Face, pose, and surroundings can divide the viewer's attention in different ways. A constant interest remains in how pictorial construction allows us to sense the presence of a specific person, without replacing observation with a fictional story.

## Clothing Extends the Figure’s Form

In "Portrait of Lena," the folds of the red garment run along the arms and torso, while the cool wall remains more open. Their contrast helps maintain a focused pose. In "Portrait of Sasha," the white blouse and brown waistcoat create clearer planes; a blue outline connects the shoulder, elbow, and hand. The artist chooses different levels of detail, but in both cases, the clothing contributes to the construction of the figure.

On Dias Rakhmatov's face, short, light, warm brushstrokes converge around the eyes and nose. Their directions convey volume without smoothly filling in the form. Towards the clothing, the brushwork becomes broader, with fewer details. This difference draws the eye back to the face. The portrait maintains a connection to a specific person through selection: one area is painted in greater detail, while another supports it with larger relationships.

When comparing works, it's helpful to observe this shift in scale. The face and figure require different distances, as do a single brushstroke and the entire canvas. In Valikhodjaev's portraiture, human presence is revealed through the coordination of these levels. Each image has its own structure: the proximity of the model, the width of the free field, the direction of the pose, the role of surrounding objects.

## About the artist

Ibragim Valikhodjaev is an artist and sculptor from Uzbekistan, an academician of the Academy of Arts of Uzbekistan, and the chairman of the Union of Artists of Uzbekistan. He works as a curator, expert, teacher, and organiser of international projects; he founded the Valikhodjaev Gallery House. [Biography and CV](<https://ibragim-valikhodjaev.blogspot.com/2026/10/blog-post.html>) .

Ibragim Valikhodjaev's works are held in the Yu Shun Art Museum (China, Harbin), in the collection of the Maltepe Municipality (Turkey, Istanbul), and also in a private collection in Moscow, Russia.
