A woman in a green dress holds a small handbag. A seated figure nearby examines a patterned object; skullcaps are laid out in the foreground. If you linger on this section of the "Namangan Suite," the larger painting temporarily transforms into a scene of several human encounters. Then, the white headscarves and brightly coloured clothing lead us further, to neighbouring groups.

Ibragim Valikhodjaev was born in Namangan. In his Namangan series, he was fascinated by the people of the bazaar, their interactions, and their everyday life. "Namangan Suite" allows us to see how observations of this environment are transformed into painting. Everyday details are linked to the length of the canvas, the distribution of figures, and the rich colour palette. To feel this connection, it's worth repeatedly switching back and forth between the entire image and its details.

## A panorama that can be entered from any side

The horizontal format unfolds the crowded scene across the canvas. The figures stand and sit, partially overlapping, forming small groups. White headdresses appear at varying heights. Their alternation links the edges of the painting, while the red, yellow, and green sections of clothing lend their own unique intensity to individual scenes.

In such a composition, the gaze chooses a route. It can begin with small objects at the bottom, move up to hands and faces, and then move on to the next light silhouette. The painting offers several routes for the eye to follow. As we examine it gradually, we notice both the structure of the crowd and the differences between individual characters.

Women occupy a particularly prominent place in Valikhodjaev’s bazaar paintings. His interest grew from the distinctive life of the Namangan bazaar: the women, their clothes, their fashion and their ways of gathering. Women sometimes sew dresses together and go out in groups. These details of the local setting help us read the paintings: clothing becomes a large patch of colour, while figures standing close together create recurring silhouettes and gestures. The rhythm of the composition grows from observation of this shared everyday life.

## Everyday life in the movement of hands

The faces of many characters are rendered with restraint, featuring a few dark features. This gives gestures a particularly expressive quality. The hands of the woman in green meet around her handbag; the posture of the seated figure directs attention to the object she holds. These small actions give the scene a human scale and help convey its inner workings.

The artist selects characteristic details and incorporates them into the overall composition. The green clothing becomes a large, calm form, the hands create dark accents, and the light-coloured handbag catches the eye. The recognisable action and relationships of the colour patches are perceived together. It is this connection that makes the everyday scene convincing in painting.

## Skullcap: an object and a pictorial motif

In the foreground, the skullcaps form a separate world of small forms. The white ornament stands out against the dark background, while light, yellow, and greenish headdresses appear nearby. Here, the scale of attention shifts: after the large figures, the eye begins to discern swirls, edges, and the juxtaposition of patterns.

The fragmented pattern of objects enlivens the lower edge of the painting. It echoes the embellishments of the clothing, but differs in density from the wide white scarves. These relationships allow the richness of the marketplace to be conveyed without filling every part of the canvas uniformly. Large forms maintain integrity, while small details invite lingering.

## Each group has its own pace.

On the right, white scarves and dark faces are closely juxtaposed. One silhouette leans forward, the other stretches upward, creating narrow gaps between them. This arrangement creates a sense of connection and proximity. Repetition connects the people, while the varied poses maintain the group's diversity.

Returning to the overall view, one can notice how these local relationships support the panorama. Some areas seem particularly dense, while others are revealed through the colour of clothing or a slight pause. The life of the bazaar is conveyed both through the people and through the tempo of the image itself: approaching, stopping, moving on to the next encounter.

## How everyday life becomes a lasting image

The power of "Namangan Suite" is revealed upon closer inspection. We recognise people and objects, then notice how precisely they are assembled on the surface. Everyday action is given a clear silhouette; clothing binds the group together with colour; a repeating scarf connects the distant parts of the canvas.

The bazaar theme gave Valikhodjaev the opportunity to explore the human environment and continually seek a different compositional solution. Later, his interest in new challenges manifested itself in his exploration of the colour red in "**[RED](<https://ibragim-valikhodjaev.blogspot.com/2026/10/colour-gives-form-looking-closely-at.html>)**" and abstraction in "New View." "Namangan Suite" demonstrates one of the pillars of this exploration: attention to the living scene and the ability to impart a coherent artistic image to it.

## How the Everyday Scene Preserves a Multitude of Voices

In a multi-figure painting, it's easy to lose an individual in the impression of a crowd. Valikhodjaev gives the viewer multiple reading scales. From a distance, white scarves unite into a large, bright rhythm; up close, the turns of heads, the variations in poses, and the tilts of arms recur. Repetition creates a coherent scene, while subtle deviations keep each episode alive. The eye can settle on one character, then trace their connection to their neighbours.

Writing in San’at in 2012, Oksana Kadysheva linked the artist's bazaar theme to the everyday lives of his contemporaries. This provides a useful context for "Namangan Suite": cultural details enter the image alongside the people's actions. The skullcap exists as both a trade item and a patterned patch of colour; the headscarf signifies clothing and is part of the larger composition. The painterly form allows for both meanings to be conveyed simultaneously. A single object continues the everyday narrative even when it becomes part of the overall colour scheme.

## About the artist

Ibragim Valikhodjaev is an artist and sculptor from Uzbekistan, an academician of the Academy of Arts of Uzbekistan, and the chairman of the Union of Artists of Uzbekistan. He also works as a curator, expert, teacher, and organiser of international projects; he founded the Valikhodjaev Gallery House. ## From a diploma subject to a recognisable signature

The bazaar attracted Valikhodjaev during the final years of his studies and became the subject of his diploma work. Beginning in realist observation, that interest later expanded into large multi-figure canvases. Duppi Bazaar of 2002 and the painting of 2004 show the theme already developing: crowded figures, recurring pale garments and the small patterns of merchandise form an independent pictorial structure. The bazaar would become one of his recognisable signatures, while curiosity continued to lead him towards other subjects, abstraction and sculpture.
